Specifically, we want to relate the **angles** to the **sides**. For example, if we know a right triangle has shorter leg length of 2, and one angle of 30 degrees, what are the other angles and sides? Can we even determine them?

<BTW_R>Well, think about this: **scaling up or down a triangle both <P>keeps its angles</P> and <P>scales its sides by a common factor</P>.**<Fragment slot="btw">These scaled triangles are called <V>similar triangles</V>.</Fragment></BTW_R> Also, one angle in a right triangle is already 90 degrees. That means if you know one other angle, then you can figure out what all 3 angles are, which means you know exactly how the triangle looks. <S>(The third angle is $180^\circ - 90^\circ-\text{known angle}$, since angles sum up to 180 degrees in a triangle.)</S> The only information you don't have is how the right triangle is _scaled_. You could be dealing with any number of similar triangles!

\[images of similar triangles of different scales, with same angles & orientation\]

If we want to relate the angles in a right triangle to the sides, we better do so in a way that **doesn't depend on which size triangle we pick with those angles**.

Then, it's natural to look at the <P>**ratios**</P> of sides. Let there be an angle $x$ in a right triangle. 
<ul>
<li>Label the leg across, or opposite, from angle $x$ as the <Op>opposite leg</Op>.</li>
<li>The leg closer to angle $x$ is the <Ad>adjacent leg</Ad>.</li>
<li>And the longest side is the <Hy>hypotenuse</Hy>.</li>
</ul>

\[pic of above\]

Then we can let the <V>sine</V> of angle $x$, or $\V{\sin x}$, denote the ratio of the length of the <Op>opposite</Op> leg to the length of the <Hy>hypotenuse</Hy>.

<RBW>
<ASK_II>
    $$ 
    \sin(x) = \frac{\text{\Op{opposite leg's length}}}{\text{\Hy{hypotenuse's length}}}
    $$
    <Fragment slot="askew">
        <span style="color: var(--og-purple);">Sine</span> : simple definition
    </Fragment>
</ASK_II>
</RBW>

\[pic with example: triangle with 30 degrees, shorter leg length of 2, hyp length of 4\]

In the above example with our $30$-degree right triangle, if we actually sat down and built a triangle with those measurements, it would have a <Hy>hypotenuse</Hy> of $4$. Therefore we say $\sin (30^\circ) = \dfrac24 = \dfrac12$.

This makes sense to say because if we took any other right triangle with angle $30^\circ$, and looked at the ratio of the length of the <Op>opposite</Op> leg to the length of the <Hy>hypotenuse</Hy>, we'd get $\frac12$. <S>_All the right triangles with an angle of $\I{30}$ degrees are similar to each other—i.e., scaled versions of each other._</S> Thus <P>**each angle $\B x$ has exactly one ratio $\B \sin$**</P>. This is just what we need!

We can similarly define the <V>cosine</V> of angle x, or $\V{\cos x}$, to be the length ratio of the <Ad>adjacent</Ad> leg over the <Hy>hypotenuse</Hy>.

<RBW>
<ASK_II>
    $$
    \cos(x)=\frac{\text{\Ad{adjacent leg's length}}}{\text{\Hy{hypotenuse's length}}}
    $$
    <Fragment slot="askew">
        <span style="color: var(--og-purple);">Cosine</span> : simple definition
    </Fragment>
</ASK_II>
</RBW>

\[pic of triangle with angle y, sides 3,4,5\]

<DRP>
<span slot="name"><Formally /></span>

For any angle $x$, where $0<x<\pi/2$, we consider the right triangle ABC with $\angle A=x \ \text{radians}$ and $\angle C=\pi/2\ \text{radians}=90^\circ$. Then, we define the sine function ($\sin:(0,\pi/2) \longrightarrow (0,1)$) as $\frac{BC}{AB}$ and the cosine function ($\cos:(0,\pi/2) \longrightarrow (0,1)$) as $\frac{AC}{AB}$. <Tn> _What are <a href="https://www.mathsisfun.com/geometry/radians.html" target="_blank">radians</a>?_ <span slot="tip">A unit of angle measurement common in more advanced math. <b>$\mathbf{2\pi}$ radians is one full turn, that is, $\mathbf{360^\circ}$.</b><br/><br/>In some sense, radians are the most "mathematical" unit of angle measurement. Click the link on the word "radians" to see more!</span> </Tn>

This is the simpler definition of sine and cosine for right triangle geometry. It will be expanded as we learn more!
</DRP>

In the triangle above, what is $\sin{y}$? What about $\cos{y}$?

<SOL>
Note that this is a right triangle, so we can use our formulas for $\sin$ and $\cos$. 

We see that, _relative to angle_ $y$, the side with length $\Op3$ is <Op>opposite</Op> and the side with length $\Ad4$ is <Ad>adjacent</Ad>. The <Hy>hypotenuse</Hy> of this right triangle has length $\Hy5$, so $\sin y = \dfrac \Opp\Hyp = \boxed{\frac35}$ and $\cos y = \dfrac \Adj\Hyp = \boxed{\frac45}$.

<br/><S>If we had picked any _similar_ right triangle—i.e., any other right triangle that this angle $y$ could make—the sides would simply be a scaled version of $3$, $4$, and $5$, and the ratios $\sin y$ and $\cos y$ would remain the same.</S>
</SOL>

<PRB>
Calculate $\sin(45^\circ)$ and $\cos(45^\circ)$. <S>_You only need some basic geo knowledge for this!_</S>
</PRB>

<SOL>
We need to draw a right triangle, as before, with one angle of $45\Deg$.

But note: the two non-right angles in a right triangle have to add to $90\Deg$ <S>(so the whole thing adds to $180\Deg$)</S>, meaning that if one of them measures $45\Deg$, the other also measures $45\Deg$, and we have an <P>**isosceles**</P> right triangle. <S>(That's "**base angles theorem**"—2 equal angles in a triangle means 2 equal sides, which is an isosceles triangle.)</S>
 
\[Image\]

Then, since this is isosceles, the two legs have the same length. If we call it $x$, then by the Pythagorean theorem, the <Hy>hypotenuse</Hy> has length $\sqrt{x^2 + x^2}=\sqrt{2x^2} = \Hy {x\sqrt{2}}$ . (Or you might just recognize this configuration as the <P>$45^\circ\text{-}45^\circ\text{-}90^\circ$</P> triangle, which has sides in ratio $x\text-x\text-x\sqrt2$.)

Now we have all the info we need: $\sin 45^\circ = \dfrac{\Opp}{\Hyp} = \dfrac{x}{x\sqrt2} = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt2}$, and so is $\cos45^\circ = \dfrac{\Adj}{\Hyp} = \dfrac{x}{x\sqrt2} = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt2}$. These are often written as $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt2}=\dfrac{\sqrt2}{\sqrt2\sqrt2}=\dfrac{\sqrt2}2$ to "rationalize the denominator".

<RCT>
$\sin45^\circ=\cos45^\circ=\dfrac{\sqrt2}2$
<div slot="and"> 
    $\sin\dfrac\pi4=\cos\dfrac\pi4=\dfrac{\sqrt2}2$
</div>
</RCT>

<br/><S>PS: Remember, every triangle with an angle of $45$ degrees has the same side ratios, so we could have just picked one that was convenient! For example, picking a triangle with legs of length $1$ would lead us to get the hypotenuse as $\sqrt{1^2+1^2}=\sqrt2$ and our $\sin$ and $\cos$ values would be $\frac{1}{\sqrt2}=\frac{\sqrt2}2$, as before.</S>
</SOL>

<HNT>
Draw an equilateral triangle. Its three internal angles are congruent—what is their degree measure? What if you split the triangle in half, forming two right triangles?
</HNT>

<SOL>
As before, we make a right triangle with an angle of $30$ degrees. Note the other angle must measure $90^\circ-30^\circ=60^\circ$.

\[Image\]

It's not immediately obvious what we might do here, and there are multiple approaches. I find this one especially nice: We note that this <P>$30^\circ\text{-}60^\circ\text{-}90^\circ$</P> triangle can actually be found inside a friendly triangle, the <P>**equilateral triangle**</P>.

\[Image\]

An equilateral triangle has three equal sides, so it has three equal angles, which add to $180\Deg$ like all triangles, so each measures $60\Deg$. Now _splitting the triangle in half_, i.e., drawing its altitude from one vertex (here, vertex $A$), we form two right triangles ($\triangle ABD$ and $\triangle ACD$), each having an angle of $60\Deg$, and thus, an angle of $30\Deg$ as well. This is exactly what we want!

Now let's assume the equilateral triangle has side length $2$. <S>(Again, we could use a variable, like $x$, but no need because we are finding _ratios_ anyways.)</S> Therefore the length of the <Hy>hypotenuses</Hy> of the two right triangles we've made is $\Hy2$. ($AB=AC=2$.)

The height split the equilateral triangle perfectly in two, so the short legs of the two right triangles have length $\frac12$ of $2$, or $1$. ($BD=CD=1$.) <S>(More formally, we might say the _altitude_ of an equilateral triangle coincides with its _median_, so the perpendicular from vertex $A$, $AD$, is also the median from $A$, i.e. $D$ is a midpoint of $BC$.)</S>

Finally, we use Pythag to determine the longer leg of these two triangles: $\sqrt{2^2-1^2}=\sqrt{4-1}=\sqrt3$.

We don't need both triangles, so let's focus on $\triangle ACD$. Specifically, we'll focus on the $30^\circ$ angle to get the sine and cosine of $30$ degrees. Can you see on the diagram that $\sin(30^\circ)=\dfrac{CD}{AC}=\frac12$? And $\cos(30^\circ)=\dfrac{AD}{AC}=\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2}$?

And focusing on the $60^\circ$ angle in $\triangle ACD$, we get $\sin(60^\circ)=\dfrac{AD}{AC}=\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2}$ and $\cos(60^\circ)=\dfrac{CD}{AC}=\frac{1}{2}$. I hope you see that it's no coincidence we get repeat values! Here, $\sin(30^\circ)=\cos(60^\circ)$ and $\sin(60^\circ)=\cos(30^\circ)$. _We're finding the same ratio, just from a different perspective._ <BTW_R>In general, <P>$\B{\sin(x) = \cos(90-x)}$ **and** $\B{\cos(x) = \sin(90-x)}$</P>. <Fragment slot="btw"><S>_This is why it's called the "co"sine — it is the sine of the "co"mplementary angle! (Complementary angles, recall, add up to $90\Deg$.)_</S></Fragment></BTW_R>

<RCT>
$$\sin30^\circ=\dfrac{1}2,\quad\sin60^\circ=\dfrac{\sqrt3}2\\ \cos30^\circ=\dfrac{\sqrt3}2,\quad \cos60^\circ=\dfrac12$$
<div slot="and">
    $\sin\dfrac\pi 6=\dfrac{1}2,\quad\sin\dfrac\pi3=\dfrac{\sqrt3}2\\ \cos\dfrac\pi6=\dfrac{\sqrt3}2,\quad \cos\dfrac\pi3=\dfrac12$
</div>
</RCT>

PS: There is another nice way to go about this that involves drawing the _median_ of the $30^\circ\text{-}60^\circ\text{-}90^\circ$ triangle from the right angle. (A median is a segment that goes from one vertex of a triangle to the midpoint of the opposite side.) If you wish, see if you can do it that way.

Also, munch on this... a $45^\circ\text{-}45^\circ\text{-}90^\circ$ triangle is half of a square, just as a this $30^\circ\text{-}60^\circ\text{-}90^\circ$ triangle is half of an equilateral triangle.

<Key/><Challenge/> Prove $\sin^2x + \cos^2x=1$.<S><Note>That is, prove that for all angles $x$ (where x is strictly between $0^\circ$ and $90^\circ$), this relation holds.</Note><BTW_R>By the way...<Fragment slot="btw">Note that $(\sin(x))^2$, the square of $\sin x$, is written $\sin^2x$.</Fragment></BTW_R></S>

<HNT>
Draw a right triangle with a general angle of $x$ and a hypotenuse of $1$. Use Pythag.
</HNT>

<SOL>
We draw a general right triangle, $\triangle ABC$, with right angle at vertex $C$, angle $x$ at vertex $A$, and angle $90^\circ-x$ at vertex $B$.

\[Image\]

Then $\sin x=\dfrac{BC}{AB}$ and $\cos x=\dfrac{AC}{AB}$. Also, Pythag tells us that the sum of the squares of the leg lengths equals the square of the hypotenuse length: i.e., $AC^2+BC^2=AB^2$.

Putting it all together: $\sin^2 x+\cos^2x=\left(\dfrac{BC}{AB}\right)^2 + \left(\dfrac{AC}{AB}\right)^2 = \dfrac{BC^2}{AB^2}+\dfrac{AC^2}{AB^2}=\dfrac{BC^2+AC^2}{AB^2}$, which, by Pythag, equals $\dfrac{AB^2}{AB^2}=1$. So $\sin^2x+\cos^2x=1$. Yay!

_Alternatively_, we could simply say, "Without loss of generality, let $AB = 1$" since sine and cosine do not depend on what the exact values of the sides are, just the ratios between them. In other words, **we _fix_ the hypotenuse to be 1**, so that $\Opp^2 + \Adj^2=1^2=1$ by Pythag. And we know $\sin x=\Opp/1=\Opp$ and $\cos x=\Adj/1=\Adj$. So $\sin^2x+\cos^2x=1$. Simple as that!

<RCT>
The <V>Pythagorean Identity</V>: $\B{\sin^2(x)+\cos^2(x)=1}$
</RCT>

I cannot stress how important this identity is. It shows up again and again!
</SOL>

### it was circles all along

What about the sine and cosine of angles that _don't fit into a right triangle_? Right now we can't find $\sin90^\circ$ or $\cos135^\circ$ or anything like that. Are these even defined?

\[Img of right triangle attempted to be made with 135 degrees with caption, _... uh, it isn't working_.\]

Well, _they can be_! This is when I admit that **I've actually lied—a teensy bit—about sine and cosine**. The definitions I gave before are correct, but _only for angles strictly between 0 and 90 degrees_. However, mathematicians actually define sine and cosine to have a <P>**domain of all real numbers**</P>, meaning you can plug any number into those functions and get a meaningful answer.

In generalizing, we turn back to the Pythagorean identity.

<RBW>
$$
\text{For all angles }\theta\text{, }\sin^2\theta+\cos^2\theta=1.
$$
</RBW>

Notice what this implies. Remember, if I travel $x$ meters east and $y$ meters north, the total distance I travel is $\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$ meters (that's Pythag). Therefore, the graph of $\sqrt{x^2+y^2} = 1$ on the plane of points $(x,y)$ looks like _all points that take a total distance of 1 unit to get to_. <BTW_R>We often write this equation as<Fragment slot="btw">(We've squared both sides here. Usually you need to take precautions when you do that, but here we don't have to worry since since both sides are always nonnegative: for all real $x$ and $y$, $x^2+y^2\geq0$ and $1\geq0$.)</Fragment></BTW_R>

$$
x^2+y^2=1.
$$

In other words, the points (x, y) that satisfy the above equation are **all points on the plane that are a distance of 1 from the origin (0,0)**. This forms a **_circle_**, specifically the <V>unit circle</V> (the circle with radius 1, centered at the origin).

\[Hm... I wonder what values for these sines would make sense...\]

This makes sense! Before, we thought of sine and cosine as the leg lengths of some right triangle divided by the <Hy>hypotenuse</Hy> length. When the <Hy>hypotenuse</Hy> had length 1, the sine was simply the length of the <Op>opposite</Op> leg, and the cosine the length of the <Ad>adjacent</Ad> leg.

So when we keep our <Hy>hypotenuse</Hy> fixed at 1, <P>**varying the angle sweeps out an arc of a circle with radius 1**</P>. We can simply <P>**complete the circle**</P>.

\[interactive element: completing the circle\]

Here is our new definition of sine and cosine:

For any angle $\theta$, we say $\sin\theta$ equals the **$\B y$-coordinate** of the point which is at angle $\theta$ around the **unit circle**, _starting at (1,0) and going counterclockwise_. We say $\cos\theta$ is the **$\B x$-coordinate** of that point.

<DRP>
<span slot="name"><Formally /></span>

For _**any**_ angle $\theta$, consider the point on the unit circle that is $\theta$ radians counterclockwise from the positive x-axis. We define $\sin \theta$ ($\sin: \R \longrightarrow \R$) to be the y-coordinate of this point, and $\cos \theta$ ($\cos: \R \longrightarrow \R$) to be the x-coordinate.

If $\theta \geq2\pi$, we wrap around the circle. If $\theta <0$, we travel clockwise.
</DRP>

<Spa height="4"/>

<PRB>
Let's gut check this new definition. Before, we had sine and cosine ranging between $0$ and $1$, exclusive (why?). Now, our sine and cosine range inclusively between $-1$ and $1$—so we've expanded the range a bit, but for good reason.  

Also, we knew $\sin(t) = \cos(90-t)$. Explain why this is still true visually with our new definitions.
</PRB>
